Latency Hiding: Why Speculation Wins

Chain-of-Thought made models smarter and slower. The fix is not better prompting — it is refusing to reason serially in the first place. Expanded from SDRC [5] and SyS-Fused [6].

Consider how a Chain-of-Thought system solves (A×B) + (C×D). It computes A×B, waits, computes C×D, waits, adds. Every step blocks on the previous one, even though the two products are completely independent. We have built machines capable of parallel computation and taught them to think in a single file line.

Speculative execution flips the contract. Instead of waiting, you decompose the problem into a dependency graph and dispatch the independent parts simultaneously — speculatively, optimistically, in parallel — then reconcile. In SDRC, an orchestrator splits the task and hands each sub-problem to a specialized expert network that runs at the same time as its siblings.

The part that makes it work: residue correction

Parallel speculation is the easy half. The hard half is learning. Backpropagating one diffuse error signal from the final answer through a pile of parallel experts tells each of them almost nothing about its mistake.

SDRC's answer is direct residue correction: each expert receives a dedicated, high-quality error signal for its own sub-task. Every module knows exactly how wrong it was, and why. In a from-scratch benchmark on multi-step arithmetic, that difference alone let SDRC converge to a better final solution than the sequential CoT baseline.

The same trick everywhere

Once you see the pattern — replace the synchronous barrier with speculation plus a local correction signal — it appears everywhere:

  • Reasoning: SDRC — parallel experts with per-task residue signals.
  • Optimization: SyS-Fused — speculation fused into the hardware-level optimization loop to hide latency entirely.
  • Decoding: SDRC-style speculative decoding — draft tokens in parallel, verify in one pass.
  • Sequence modeling: vTRN — the ultimate version: remove the global synchronous step altogether.

Serial thought was never a law of nature. It was an implementation detail we mistook for the architecture.
